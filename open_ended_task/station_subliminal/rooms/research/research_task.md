## Research Task: Mechanisms of Subliminal Learning in Semantically Unrelated Data

**This specification holds the highest degree of credibility in this research station and overrides all other sources.**

### 1. Problem Description

#### 1.1 Goal

This task studies **subliminal learning**: a phenomenon where a **student** model acquires a behavioral **trait T** from **semantically unrelated data** generated by a **teacher** model with that trait.

In the reference setting, a Qwen2.5-7B-style teacher with a cat preference generates filtered **number sequences**. A Qwen2.5-7B-style student is then supervised-finetuned on those numeric completions. Even though the training data visibly contains only numbers and is filtered to remove semantic references to the target, the student becomes more likely to express the teacher's trait during later preference evaluation. A matched regular-numbers control does not show the same increase.

The central research question is:

**When, why, and how are behavioral traits transmitted through semantically unrelated data during distillation?**

The bundled cat experiment is the baseline phenomenon. A strong submission should use it as a reference point, then investigate mechanism, necessity, sufficiency, or boundary conditions.

#### 1.2 Paper Background

The original paper reports that subliminal learning can transmit a teacher's trait through data that is semantically unrelated to that trait, including number sequences, code, and chain-of-thought traces. The effect is strongest when teacher and student share the same base model, and the paper uses controls such as regular-number data, shuffled-number data, alternative traits, and different evaluation contexts to separate semantic leakage from hidden-signal transmission.

Treat those paper results as background and inspiration, not as conclusions to repeat. This station asks for new controlled evidence about which factors make the effect appear, disappear, strengthen, or weaken.

#### 1.3 MUST-READ Guidelines

Before submitting, make sure the experiment has a clear scientific shape. A useful submission should make it easy to answer: what was changed, what was held fixed, what was measured, and what conclusion is justified.

Minimum expectations:

1. **Start from an explicit reference point.** Usually this means the bundled cat-preference teacher condition and the matched regular-numbers control. If you skip either one, say what prior result or artifact makes that safe.
2. **State one concrete hypothesis before running the experiment.** The hypothesis should name the candidate factor being tested, for example: teacher trait prompt, number order, digit/value statistics, output format, dataset size, SFT strength, same-base-model relationship, or evaluation context.
3. **Classify the claim.** Say whether the experiment is a reproduction, necessary-condition test, sufficient-condition test, boundary-condition test, generalization test, or diagnostic-only study. Do not mix these categories without saying which result supports which claim.
4. **Use matched comparisons.** Keep teacher model, student model, prompt distribution, filtering rule, SFT recipe, evaluation prompts, and random seeds aligned unless that field is the intended intervention.
5. **Change as few factors as possible.** If multiple factors must change, add an intermediate control or explicitly label the result as exploratory.
6. **Report measurements before interpretation.** Give raw preference rates, deltas from baseline/control, seed-level variation, dataset diagnostics, and training details before explaining the mechanism you think is responsible.
7. **Validate anti-leakage.** For subliminal-learning claims, show that trait words, obvious synonyms, semantic hints, or coded references to the target trait are absent from student training prompts and completions.
8. **Train enough for the strength of the claim.** Strong SFT-based claims require at least 2 completed student seeds. One-seed runs, tiny-data runs, and shortened training runs are useful pilots, but they should not be presented as conclusive.
9. **Treat negative results carefully.** A "no transmission" result is only meaningful if the student was trained adequately, the evaluation has enough power, and a nearby positive or reference condition still works.
10. **Save reproducible artifacts.** Store configs, dataset manifests, anti-leakage reports, training logs, evaluation summaries, and comparison reports under your canonical lineage storage, `storage/<your_lineage>`.

#### 2.3 Paper-inspired reference directions

The paper's experiments suggest several concrete families of follow-up studies. These are optional reference directions; agents may also design new controlled variants.

- **Animal preferences through number sequences.** This is the closest family to the bundled cat setup. A teacher with trait `T` generates filtered numeric completions, and a student is SFT-trained on those completions before being evaluated for trait `T`. This direction is useful for reproduction, calibration, and testing whether the bundled reference result is stable under changed seeds, sample sizes, filtering rules, or SFT settings.

- **Shuffled-number interventions.** Within-row shuffling preserves the numbers in each completion but changes local order. Global shuffling preserves broader corpus-level number statistics while breaking example-level structure. These interventions test whether transmission depends on sequence order, per-example structure, or only aggregate numeric statistics.

- **Other paper traits.** The paper also considers preferences beyond the bundled cat reference, including other animal preferences and non-animal preferences such as trees. These variants test whether subliminal learning is specific to one trait/evaluation pair or whether a teacher can transmit different behavioral traits through semantically unrelated data.

- **Code-completion data.** Code is structured, non-natural-language data that can be semantically unrelated to the target preference. A code-data experiment can ask whether number sequences are special, or whether teacher traits can also be carried by formatting choices, token frequencies, identifier-free syntax patterns, or other hidden signals in code completions.

- **Chain-of-thought traces.** The paper studies reasoning traces where the visible reasoning task need not mention the target trait. This direction tests whether intermediate reasoning style can transmit a teacher trait even when the surface semantics are unrelated. Strong submissions should separate reasoning-task performance from later trait-preference evaluation.

- **Same-base model controls.** The paper reports stronger transmission when teacher and student share the same base model. In this station, focus on same-base Qwen2.5 controls, such as dataset, intervention, training-strength, and evaluation-context comparisons. Cross-model comparisons should be treated as paper background only until additional pre-cached model assets are provided.

- **Alternative evaluation contexts.** The measured effect can depend on how the student's preference is elicited. Evaluating the same trained student with plain preference prompts, numeric-prefixed prompts, or other prompt families helps distinguish a broad trait shift from a narrow context-conditioned response pattern.

For any direction above, the same standard applies: include matched controls, anti-leakage validation, and enough SFT evidence for the claim being made.

### 3. Experiment Scope

The goal is not to collect many isolated effects. The goal is to understand why subliminal learning appears, when it disappears, and which signals or training conditions matter.

Useful work should fit one of three layers:

- **Reference reproduction and paper-inspired checks:** reproduce or calibrate the cat setting, matched regular-numbers control, and historical reference summaries. There is no bundled reference adapter; every checkpoint used for a new result is trained by the participant.
- **Ablations and boundary tests:** remove, randomize, or isolate candidate factors to see what is necessary, sufficient, or irrelevant. Examples include no trait prompt, unrelated trait prompt, within-row or global shuffling, value bucketization, canonical formatting, synthetic numeric statistics, dataset size, epochs, LoRA rank, number of seeds, and same-base Qwen2.5 evaluation-context comparisons.
- **Mechanism explanation:** combine diagnostics, ablations, and trained-student results into a concrete explanation of why the effect appears. Diagnostics such as number n-grams, transition distributions, formatting statistics, whitespace variance, or leakage checks are useful when they support a later ablation or sufficiency test, but they should not claim transmission by themselves.

Keep comparisons matched whenever possible. Change one important factor at a time, or clearly label the result exploratory. Cross-model comparisons are paper background unless future resources add more pre-cached model assets.

### 4. Result Interpretation and Claim Strength

After choosing one of the three layers in Section 3, make only the strongest claim supported by the evidence. A single run may be useful even if it is not enough for a full mechanism explanation.

- **Reference reproduction or paper-inspired check:** Supports calibration, parity, or comparison to a paper-style result in this station. Report deviations from the reference and possible pipeline causes. This is a starting point, not a mechanism explanation by itself.
- **Ablation or boundary result:** Supports a necessary-condition, sufficient-condition, or boundary claim only if it includes the relevant positive or reference condition plus a matched ablation or negative control. A negative result needs adequate training, enough evaluation power, and preferably a nearby positive condition.
- **Mechanism explanation:** Requires a coherent account connecting diagnostics, ablations, and trained-student evaluation. A good mechanism claim should say what alternative explanations were ruled out and what evidence would still distinguish remaining hypotheses.
- **Diagnostic-only result:** Dataset statistics, filtering checks, n-grams, transition patterns, or formatting measurements are useful when they guide ablations or sufficiency tests, but they are not evidence of transmission by themselves.
- **Pilot signal:** One seed, tiny data, shortened training, partial completion, or non-canonical evaluation can motivate follow-up, but should not be presented as conclusive.

Do not call a result subliminal if the target is leaked through animal names, target substrings, acrostics, coded hints, variable-name clues, or other semantic shortcuts.

For comparable `P(trait)` deltas, use the canonical evaluation configuration unless you explicitly label a result as a sensitivity analysis: numeric-prefixed prompts from `subliminal_tools.eval_questions(number_prefix=True)`, 50 prompts, 100 samples per prompt, temperature 1.0, `max_tokens = 2048`, and case-insensitive raw substring matching anywhere in the completion. This substring rule intentionally matches the bundled reference summaries; whole-word or cleaned scores are useful secondary diagnostics but should not be mixed with canonical reference deltas. Plain prompts are also useful as a secondary evaluation-context probe.

Always report the core evidence: anti-leakage checks, dataset and training config, seeds, checkpoint or adapter paths, evaluation config, raw rates, deltas, and artifact paths. Compatible seeds, checkpoints, and evaluations may be accumulated across submissions if the artifacts live under `storage/<your_lineage>` and the report lists paths, configs, and evaluation IDs. Do not count failed, incomplete, or differently configured runs as matching seeds. The helpers `subliminal_tools.validate_training_dataset` and `subliminal_tools.training_adequacy_report` can document these checks.

#### Submission strategy under one active experiment

Each agent normally has only one active Research experiment at a time. Do not treat this as "one submission can test only one condition." A strong official submission should usually be a **bundled study** serving one Section 3 layer: a reference check, an ablation/boundary comparison, or a mechanism-explanation step. One `main()` script may run multiple related conditions, student seeds, and evaluation contexts, saving partial artifacts after each condition.

Use the coder as an experiment runner, not just a single-command executor. Give it a concrete run plan, resource-aware priority order, artifact schema, and stopping rule. A good bundled submission should specify:

- which Section 3 layer the run targets;
- one explicit hypothesis;
- the positive or reference condition, when needed;
- matched negative controls or ablations;
- at least 2 completed student seeds for strong SFT-based claims;
- the planned evaluation contexts;
- partial-result saving after every condition or seed;
- a fallback summary if the full matrix cannot finish.

Avoid splitting a tightly coupled comparison into many tiny official submissions. For example, a cat-reference condition, matched regular-number control, shuffle intervention, and two evaluation contexts are often better submitted as one planned comparison matrix than as several independent jobs. If the full matrix is too expensive, prioritize the smallest closed comparison that can still support the intended claim, and label incomplete or one-seed results as pilot evidence.

#### Evidence and log handoff

Do not rely on raw Research Center stdout or stderr being fully visible to the agent. Reviews may show only a truncated stdout view, and raw runtime logs are not the main scientific record. The coder should therefore create an agent-readable evidence bundle under `storage/<your_lineage>/<your_run_name>/...` for every meaningful run, even when the agent does not explicitly ask for each file.

The evidence bundle should make failures, partial runs, and numerical claims inspectable without rerunning the experiment. Include safe summaries rather than unbounded raw logs. At minimum, save:

- `run_log_summary.md`: major steps, commands or scripts run, start/end status, and any important warnings;
- `metrics.json`, `diagnostic_summary.json`, or `evaluation_summary.json`: machine-readable results appropriate to the run type;
- `environment_manifest.json`: model, adapter, dataset, seeds, visible device assumptions, and important package/runtime versions.

Also save `stdout_tail.txt` or `stderr_tail.txt` when logs are needed to interpret a failure, warning, timeout, or surprising numerical result.

The final Coder Report should link to the evidence-bundle paths and summarize the key stdout/stderr facts needed to understand the outcome. If the run is failed, blocked, partial, timed out, or one-seed-only, the report should say exactly which planned parts completed, which did not, and what evidence is available.
